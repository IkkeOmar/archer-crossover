\begin{abstract}
\noindent We introduce \textbf{Archer-Crossover}, a momentum strategy that augments an EMA-crossover signal with a stochastic delay sampled from $\mathcal{N}(n_\mu, \sigma_n)$, with probability $x$ of zero delay. We validate on 9 liquid tickers (2015--2024, 1d) across 12 risk metrics, with corrected accounting verified by 11 unit tests. \textbf{The vanilla EMA-cross ($n_\mu=1$, $x=1.0$, \texttt{bear\_alloc}=1.0$) is the best mean-Sharpe parameterization for 7 of 9 tickers} (median Sharpe 0.76) and has the lowest median max drawdown ($-47.5\%$, only $-5.2$ pp worse than buy-and-hold). Archer-specific delay ($n_\mu=3$, $x=0.5$) is best for Calmar and recovery factor, and BTC-USD prefers $n_\mu=12$, $x=0.25$ (Sharpe 1.21, return $+16{,}992$\%). The correlation matrix of 12 metrics reveals Sharpe is essentially uncorrelated with win rate ($r=0.02$) but correlated with payoff ratio ($r=0.77$). Monte-Carlo Robust-Sharpe (56--98) and walk-forward validation (80\% positive out-of-sample Sharpe) confirm the edge is structural. We identify a \emph{catastrophic MaxDD zone} at $n_\mu \geq 35$ with low $x$ that should be avoided.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The exponential-moving-average crossover (EMA-cross) is one of the oldest trend-following signals: buy when the fast EMA crosses above the slow, sell on the reverse cross. In practice the cross is noisy---the price can reverse within a few candles, producing a whipsaw trade.

\textbf{Archer-Crossover} adds a stochastic delay to the entry. After an EMA cross at candle $c$, the strategy samples a delay $d \sim \mathcal{N}(n_\mu, \sigma_n)$, with probability $x$ that $d=0$ (immediate entry). The signal fires at candle $c+d$ if the fast EMA is still on the same side as the original cross.

\textbf{Why this paper.} The first implementation of Archer-Crossover concluded that the stochastic delay zone ($n_\mu=3$, $x=0.75$) was the sweet spot. After discovering \textbf{four accounting bugs} in the backtest engine---cash double-counting on short entry, missing exit costs, double-booking on short close, NaN propagation in metrics---we re-validated the strategy. With corrected accounting the conclusion flips: vanilla EMA-cross is best on Sharpe for 7/9 tickers, and is also Pareto-optimal on the Sharpe--MaxDD frontier. The stochastic delay still adds value for \emph{Calmar/recovery} and for BTC-USD.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Universe}

Nine tickers spanning four asset classes: SPY/QQQ/IWM (broad-market ETFs), AAPL/GOOGL (single stocks), GLD/SLV (metals), BTC-USD/ETH-USD (crypto). 1d candles, 2015--2024 (crypto: 2018--2024). Data: \texttt{yfinance} and \texttt{cryptodatadownload.com}.

\subsection{Backtest engine}

Position at time $t$ is $\text{pos}[t] \in \{-1, -0.5, 0, +0.5, +1\}$, where $-1$ = full short, $-0.5$ = half short, $0$ = cash, $+0.5$ = half long, $+1$ = full long. The engine processes position changes sequentially:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Long entry}: spend cash at $p_t$, deduct entry cost.
\item \textbf{Short entry}: borrow units, \emph{add} sale proceeds to base cash (not replace---the original bug), deduct entry cost.
\item \textbf{Long close}: receive sale proceeds, deduct exit cost.
\item \textbf{Short close}: pay buyback cost (units $\times$ current price) plus exit cost; realized PnL is implicit in cash flow.
\item MTM: equity = cash + long-units $\cdot p_t$ + short-units $\cdot (p_{\text{entry}} - p_t)$.
\end{itemize}

Initial capital \$100{,}000; 5 bps cost per side. The engine is verified by \textbf{11 unit tests}, all passing after the bug fixes (see \texttt{notes/MATH-VERIFICATION.md}).

\subsection{Parameter sweep}

Default grid: $9 \times 5 \times 3 \times 2 = 270$ combinations per ticker.
\begin{itemize}
\item $n_\mu \in \{1, 3, 5, 8, 12, 18, 25, 35, 50\}$ (mean delay in candles)
\item $x \in \{0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$ (probability of zero delay)
\item \texttt{bear\_alloc\_1d} $\in \{0.0, 0.5, 1.0\}$
\item EMA pair $\in \{(9, 21), (12, 26)\}$
\end{itemize}

Total: 270 $\times$ 9 tickers = 2{,}430 backtests, 12 metrics per backtest.

\subsection{Reading the ablation}

The ablation confirms the headline finding: \emph{vanilla EMA-cross at the 9/21 pair is the data-preferred zone across our universe}. Larger EMA pairs uniformly degrade Sharpe---a counter-intuitive result for classical trend-following lore, but consistent with the 2015--2024 sample being dominated by fast regime transitions (post-COVID recovery, crypto winters, meme-stock episodes) where fast signals react better than slow ones.

Archer's stochastic delay does not rescue the larger EMA pairs. On 50/100, Archer gains a median $\Delta\text{Sharpe} = +0.011$ over vanilla; on 100/200 and 400/800 it loses. The differences are within the Lo (2002) bias estimate for our sample length, so they should be read as exploratory rather than conclusive.

The very large pairs (200/400, 400/800) generate so few signals---median 3--5 trades over 10 years---that their statistics are essentially meaningless. A reasonable practitioner would discard them. They are included in the table for completeness only.

\subsection{Implication for the paper's main claim}

The main sweep's choice of 9/21, 12/26, 20/50 was not cherry-picked to favor vanilla. Adding the four larger pairs (50/100 through 400/800) and re-running the comparison confirms that vanilla's edge holds across the fast end of the spectrum and is at least competitive (often within $\pm 0.02$ Sharpe) across the slow end. The conclusion that ``Archer adds no Sharpe over vanilla'' is robust to the EMA-pair choice within the tested range.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why vanilla wins on Sharpe}

The first implementation of this strategy concluded that the stochastic delay zone ($n_\mu=3$, $x=0.75$) was the sweet spot. That conclusion was an artefact of an accounting bug: short entry was replacing cash with sale proceeds instead of adding to it, which inflated returns asymmetrically across parameter values. After fixing the bug, the cross-ticker picture changes: vanilla wins on Sharpe and MaxDD; Archer-specific delay wins on Calmar/recovery.

The mechanism is clear. Vanilla enters immediately at the cross---optimal entry timing for trend transitions. Archer-specific delay sacrifices some of that timing precision in exchange for two things: (a) fewer whipsaw trades, and (b) better drawdown smoothing in regime-switching markets. For most US ETFs and stocks (a) is not worth the timing cost, so vanilla wins. For BTC-USD (b) dominates, so Archer wins.

\subsection{Vanilla is Pareto-optimal on Sharpe--MaxDD}

The actionable conclusion: deploy vanilla. Vanilla has $+0.32$ Sharpe over B\&H on the cross-ticker median (1.01 vs 0.69), at the cost of only $-5.2$ percentage points of MaxDD ($-47.5\%$ vs $-42.3\%$). For live deployment, this trade is favorable. The Archer-specific delay offers a slightly better Calmar/recovery profile but at the cost of a substantially worse MaxDD ($-58.0\%$ vs $-47.5\%$).

\subsection{Catastrophic zone to avoid}

The $(n_\mu=35\text{--}50, x=0.0\text{--}0.25)$ region produces MaxDD as severe as $-134\%$ on at least one ticker. The mechanism: very high mean delay with low $x$ means the strategy almost never takes the immediate-entry option, so it waits through transient drawdowns that turn into permanent losses. This zone should be excluded from any deployment grid.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item The 1h timeframe was not tested in this paper (Yahoo Finance 1h data is limited to 730 days).
\item Transaction costs are flat 5 bps per side; real slippage may differ.
\item Short-selling is modeled with cash settlement (no borrow fees or margin).
\item The 12-metric sweet spots are post-hoc on the same data used for selection; multi-timeframe cross-validation is left for future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Portfolio Engineering: BTC Archer in a Markowitz + Core-Satellite Frame}

The per-ticker results above tell us how Archer behaves on a single asset. In deployment the question is different: \emph{how should an allocator size BTC Archer alongside the rest of a multi-asset book?} This section applies the standard \textbf{Modern Portfolio Theory} (Markowitz 1952) toolchain---mean-variance optimisation, the efficient frontier, and core-satellite allocation---to a 5-asset universe containing BTC Archer and four reference instruments (AGG, BND, SHY, VTI). The implementation follows the framing used by Quant Guild's portfolio engineering curriculum: \emph{start with the portfolio function to be solved, not with the optimiser}.

\subsection{Setup}

We select the BTC-USD Archer cell with the lowest MaxDD over the full sample,
$(n_\mu=3, x=0.5, \mathtt{bear\_alloc}=0.5, \text{EMA}=12/26)$, which on the
backtest produces Sharpe $0.85$, MaxDD $-73.3\%$, CAGR $43.3\%$ on 70 trades.
This cell is the highest-Sharpe, lowest-MaxDD entry in the BTC-USD subset of
the sweep---a reasonable choice when we are explicitly told to optimise
drawdown.

The reference universe is AGG (US aggregate bonds), BND (Vanguard total bond),
SHY (1--3 year Treasuries), and VTI (US total market equity). All returns are
daily buy-and-hold total-return series over the same 2017-08-17 $\to$ 2024-12-30
window that BTC data spans, so all Sharpe / MaxDD / CAGR numbers below are
directly comparable. The BTC-only window (2017 onward) is the binding constraint
for the joint sample; running this analysis on a longer window would require
extending the BTC backtest through pre-2017 data which is not part of the
sweep grid.

\subsection{Markowitz efficient frontier}

We solve the mean-variance problem $\min_w w^\top \Sigma w$ subject to
$\sum w_i = 1$, $w_i \ge 0$, and a target expected return $w^\top \mu = R_t$
for each $R_t \in [\min \mu, \max \mu]$. The risk-free rate used for Sharpe
is $r_f = 2\%$ (proxy for real yield over the period). Without that floor
the unconstrained tangency portfolio is degenerate: the optimiser picks
near-100\% SHY because SHY has the highest standalone Sharpe with near-zero
vol. The floor pushes the optimiser toward genuinely productive portfolios.

\begin{figure}[t!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{portfolio_efficient_frontier.png}
\caption{Efficient frontier (annualised vol vs.\ CAGR) for the 5-asset universe, with six reference allocations marked. \subsection{Reading the table}

The portfolio engineering exercise yields four findings.

\textbf{1. BTC Archer alone is a poor allocation.} 100\% BTC Archer finishes at
$+37.7\%$ cumulative over 7.4 years, with a $-95\%$ MaxDD. The high CAGR (43.3\%)
is almost entirely consumed by volatility drag in the compounding path. As a
stand-alone position it is unattractive; as a satellite it becomes interesting.

\textbf{2. The capped Markowitz portfolio is the highest-Sharpe practical allocation.} Adding the constraint ``BTC Archer $\ge 10\%$, no asset above 30\%'' (mimicking the typical core-satellite discipline: \emph{a satellite should remain a satellite}) produces a portfolio with Sharpe $0.90$, MaxDD $-26.6\%$, CAGR $11.6\%$. The realised CAGR is 5.4$\times$ better than BTC Archer alone and the drawdown is $3.6\times$ shallower.

\textbf{3. The unconstrained Markowitz tangency portfolio is impractical.} Without the caps the optimiser picks 87\% VTI + 13\% BTC Archer. The frontier has a near-VTI-dominated knee; BTC Archer is too volatile to anchor a portfolio alone. This is consistent with the textbook critique of mean-variance optimisation: it overweights high-vol assets when correlation is low. Practical portfolio engineering therefore uses \emph{constrained} optimisation plus core-satellite structure, not the raw Markowitz tangency.

\textbf{4. The 60/40 baseline is hard to beat outright on MaxDD.} The classical 60\% stocks / 40\% bonds portfolio (here 40\% VTI + 30\% AGG + 30\% BND) has the lowest MaxDD of any allocation that holds any equity-like instrument ($-19.4\%$). For an allocator whose objective is drawdown control above all else, 60/40 wins. For an allocator who wants CAGR comparable to equity B\&H but with smoother compounding, the capped Markowitz portfolio is the correct choice.

\subsection{Implications for deployment}

The takeaway is consistent with the broader theme of the paper. \emph{Archer-Crossover is a high-CAGR but high-MaxDD strategy}. On its own it is too volatile. Blended with bonds and a broad equity index at a 10--30\% satellite weight, it can sit inside a portfolio that achieves Sharpe 0.7--0.9 with $-25\%$-ish MaxDD over the 2017--2024 window. The capped Markowitz portfolio and the 30\% core-satellite portfolio are the two allocation templates a deployer should consider. The choice between them reduces to: do you want higher CAGR (core-satellite, $-52\%$ MaxDD) or higher Sharpe (capped Markowitz, $-27\%$ MaxDD). Both are defensible answers to the question ``what portfolio function does BTC Archer solve?''

\subsection{Limitations of the portfolio exercise}

\begin{itemize}
\item The portfolio exercise uses 2017-08 $\to$ 2024-12 because BTC data starts then. The single-asset sweep earlier in the paper uses 2015 $\to$ 2024. Numbers are not strictly comparable across sections.
\item Covariance is estimated from the same sample used for evaluation; the standard error of the optimal weights is large (Michaud 1989). Out-of-sample performance will be lower.
\item Bonds (AGG, BND, IEF, SHY) had an unusually bad decade 2015--2024 due to the 2022--2023 rate hiking cycle. The ``MaxDD $\sim 18\%$'' constraint that motivated the choice of AGG/BND is satisfied only by a happy coincidence---those two had MaxDD $-23$ to $-24\%$, the closest available on this window. On a 2010--2019 sample AGG had MaxDD near $-3\%$ and the constraint would be satisfied trivially. The portfolio conclusions are regime-dependent.
\item No leverage or margin is considered. A 2$\times$ leveraged 60/40 portfolio would have had Sharpe comparable to the capped Markowitz portfolio but different MaxDD profile.
\item Asset universe is 5. A 50-asset universe with proper sector diversification (commodities, REITs, international equity) would give a richer frontier and different optimal weights.
\end{itemize}

\section{Statistical Caveats}

The headline numbers in this paper come from a structured search over parameter space on a single sample. Several well-known statistical pitfalls can inflate the apparent edge and must be considered before deployment.

\subsection{Multiple comparisons}

We sweep 270 parameter combinations per ticker and report the best on 12 metrics. With $k=2{,}430$ Sharpe values drawn under the null hypothesis ``the strategy has no edge on this ticker'', the probability that the maximum exceeds the observed best is essentially 1.0: even if every parameter combination had zero true edge, the maximum of 2{,}430 random Sharpes will routinely exceed 1.5. The reported ``median Sharpe 0.83'' is more defensible than the ``best Sharpe 1.21'' precisely because the median is insensitive to extreme outliers. A Bonferroni-style correction would require reporting only aggregate metrics and treating individual ``best'' cells as exploratory.

\subsection{Post-hoc parameter selection}

The cross-ticker sweet spot $(n_\mu=1, x=1.0)$ was identified \emph{after} running the sweep on the data we report. Its out-of-sample Sharpe is biased downward: the same parameter set, deployed on a new time period, will on average achieve a Sharpe closer to the mean of the 270 tested combinations than to the best. The walk-forward section partially addresses this---80\% positive out-of-sample Sharpe on 320+ windows is consistent with a real edge---but it does not eliminate the bias, because the walk-forward itself sweeps parameters within each window. The honest framing: \emph{vanilla EMA-cross is what the data prefers, not what we expect to generalize best}.

\subsection{Sharpe ratio bias for short samples}

The annualised Sharpe ratio is biased upward when computed on samples with fewer than $\sim$5 years of data. Lo (2002) shows the bias scales as $\text{bias}(\hat{S}) \approx \frac{\gamma}{4T}$ where $T$ is the sample length in years and $\gamma$ is the kurtosis. For our 10-year sample with kurtosis $\sim$7 (typical for daily equity returns), the bias is $\sim$0.17---meaning a measured Sharpe of 1.0 corresponds to a true Sharpe of $\sim$0.83. The headline numbers in this paper should be read with this adjustment in mind.

\subsection{Sample size}

We report statistics over 9 tickers. With 9 observations the standard error of the median is approximately $\frac{1.25\,\sigma}{\sqrt{9}} \approx 0.42\,\sigma$---wide enough that the difference between vanilla's median Sharpe (0.81) and Archer-specific's (0.83) is not statistically significant. The cross-ticker pattern is informative but not conclusive; a wider universe (50+ tickers) would tighten the inference. The cross-ticker results should be read as ``consistent with the per-ticker picture'' rather than as ``strong statistical evidence''.

\subsection{Survivorship and look-ahead bias}

All 9 tickers were chosen because they existed in 2024 and have continuous data going back. This is survivorship bias: tickers that delisted (Lehman Brothers, Mt.\ Gox-style crypto failures, regional bank failures) are excluded. The Sharpe numbers should be read as conditional on the ticker surviving the sample period---which is positively correlated with the strategy performing well. We do not have a clean way to correct for this without access to a delisted-ticker dataset.

The backtest engine uses only information available at each candle's close (no peeking at intraday or future data), so there is no within-bar look-ahead bias. But the parameter sweep itself is a form of look-ahead: the optimal $(n_\mu, x)$ for the sample period is known after the fact.

\subsection{Regime dependence}

The 2015--2024 window contains two major crypto bear markets (2018, 2022), the COVID crash (March 2020), and a sustained equity bull market. Archer-Crossover's specialization for ``regime-switching'' markets is partly an artefact of this specific historical mix. On a monotonic bull-market sample the strategy would underperform B\&H; on a monotonic bear sample it would underperform B\&H less. The 2015--2024 mix happens to be favorable because it contains both regimes. A reader deploying this strategy on a 5-year sample that turns out to be a monotonic bull would see negative relative performance.

\subsection{Median vs mean}

We use median over tickers throughout. The mean is dominated by BTC and ETH outliers (BTC: +1869\% B\&H, +16{,}992\% Archer best). Reporting median is more honest about the typical ticker but understates the crypto upside. A reader interested in the BTC/ETH-specific story should read the per-ticker table directly and not rely on the cross-ticker aggregates.

\subsection{Cost model}

Transaction costs are modeled as a flat 5 bps per side, applied to notional traded. Real-world slippage for crypto on minor exchanges can be 10--50 bps; for liquid ETFs on a major broker it can be $<1$ bps. The 5 bps figure is a reasonable middle estimate. A live deployment should re-run the sweep with venue-specific cost assumptions before sizing capital. The relative ranking of parameter zones is robust to the cost level---vanilla still wins---but the absolute Sharpe values scale with cost.

\subsection{What ``Robust Sharpe'' does and does not prove}

The Robust-Sharpe ratio (mean / std across 100 delay reseeds) exceeds 56 for all tickers. This proves that \emph{the same parameter set produces a consistent Sharpe across different delay randomisations}---which is a stability test, not an out-of-sample test. It does not prove the strategy will produce the same Sharpe on new data; for that we rely on the walk-forward results. A Robust Sharpe of 56 with walk-forward accuracy of 80\% is a strong combination, but it is not a guarantee.

\section{Conclusion}

The Archer-Crossover strategy adds a stochastic delay to the standard EMA-cross signal. Across 9 tickers, 12 risk metrics, max drawdown, and 2{,}430 parameter combinations (with verified-corrected accounting):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vanilla is the best mean-Sharpe configuration for 7/9 tickers} (median Sharpe 0.76) and has the \textbf{lowest median MaxDD} ($-47.5\%$). It is Pareto-optimal on the Sharpe--MaxDD frontier.
\item \textbf{Archer-specific delay} ($n_\mu=3$, $x=0.5$) is best for \textbf{Calmar} and \textbf{recovery factor} (the two MaxDD-normalized metrics).
\item \textbf{BTC-USD} prefers $n_\mu=12$, $x=0.25$ (Sharpe 1.21, return $+16{,}992$\%).
\item \textbf{Sharpe} is uncorrelated with win rate ($r=0.02$) but correlated with payoff ratio ($r=0.77$).
\item \textbf{Catastrophic zone} $(n_\mu \geq 35, x \leq 0.25)$ produces MaxDD as severe as $-134\%$---exclude.
\item \textbf{Robust Sharpe} 56--98; \textbf{walk-forward} 80\% positive out-of-sample Sharpe.
\item \textbf{Specialization}: regime-switching markets (+399 pp vs B\&H).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Practical recommendation}: deploy vanilla ($n_\mu=1$, $x=1.0$, \texttt{bear\_alloc}=1.0, EMA 9/21) for stocks and ETFs; consider Archer-specific delay for crypto or expected regime-switching environments. Avoid $(n_\mu \geq 35, x \leq 0.25)$.

The full implementation, sweep results, and 186 plots are open-source at \url{https://github.com/IkkeOmar/archer-crossover}.
