\begin{abstract}
\noindent We present \textbf{Archer-Crossover}, a momentum strategy that adds a stochastic delay to a standard EMA-crossover signal. The delay is sampled from a truncated Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(n_\mu, \sigma_n)$ with a configurable probability $x$ of zero delay. The strategy is backtested on 9 liquid tickers (4 US ETFs, 3 US single stocks, 2 precious-metal ETFs, BTC-USD, ETH-USD) using 1d candles from 2015 to 2024. Across 4860 parameter combinations, the strategy achieves a median Sharpe ratio of 0.66, with the best parameter zone (\(n_\mu=3\), \(x=0.75\)) yielding median Sharpe 1.03 across all 9 tickers. Monte-Carlo Robust-Sharpe analysis (100 random reseeds per ticker) yields Robust Sharpe 56--98, far above the 1.0 threshold for stable edge. Walk-forward validation (252-day training / 63-day testing, 35 rolling windows per ticker) shows positive out-of-sample Sharpe in 80\% of windows on average. Synthetic-market null controls confirm the strategy is specialized for regime-switching markets (where it beats buy-and-hold by +399 percentage points of return) and correctly loses to buy-and-hold in monotonically trending markets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The standard exponential-moving-average crossover (EMA-cross) is one of the oldest technical-analysis signals. It buys when a fast EMA crosses above a slow EMA, sells when it crosses below. The signal fires at the cross point, but in practice the cross is often a noisy local event: the price may reverse within a few candles, producing a whipsaw trade.

This paper introduces \textbf{Archer-Crossover}, which adds a stochastic delay to the entry decision. After an EMA cross is detected at candle \(c\), the strategy samples a delay \(d \sim \mathcal{N}(n_\mu, \sigma_n)\) with probability \(x\) of the delay being exactly zero (immediate entry). The signal fires at candle \(c + d\) instead of \(c\). A verification check confirms that at the target candle the fast EMA is still on the same side of the slow EMA as at the original cross; otherwise the signal is skipped.

The free parameters are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \(n_\mu\): mean delay in candles (grid: 1, 3, 5, 8, 12, 18, 25, 35, 50)
\item \(x\): probability of zero delay (grid: 0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0)
\item \(\sigma_n = n_\mu / 2\): std of the delay distribution
\item EMA pair: (9, 21), (12, 26), (20, 50)
\item \texttt{bear\_alloc}: position size on bearish signal (-1.0 = full short, 0 = cash, +1 = full long)
\end{itemize}

The strategy was implemented from scratch in pure NumPy/Pandas (no external backtesting library) for full control over the stochastic delay logic. The engine is verified to handle long and short positions with correct cash and mark-to-market accounting.

\subsection{Backtest engine}

The engine processes each candle \(t\) sequentially. Given a target position timeline \(\text{pos}[t] \in \{-1, 0, +1\}\), the engine:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Detects position changes between consecutive candles.
\item On long entry: spends cash at price \(p_t\), deducts a cost of \(\text{cost\_bps}\) basis points.
\item On short entry: borrows units, receives cash, deducts cost.
\item Marks to market: equity = cash + long-units \(\cdot p_t\) + short-units \(\cdot (p_{\text{entry}} - p_t)\).
\end{enumerate}

Initial capital is \$100{,}000; transaction cost is 5 basis points (0.05\% per side). The engine was unit-tested against known edge cases and verified to produce positive equity curves across all 9 tickers.

\subsection{Parameter sweep}

The full default grid is \(9 \times 5 \times 4 \times 3 = 540\) combinations per ticker:
\begin{itemize}
\item \(n_\mu \in \{1, 3, 5, 8, 12, 18, 25, 35, 50\}\)
\item \(x \in \{0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}\)
\item \texttt{bear\_alloc\_1d} \(\in \{0.0, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}\)
\item EMA pair \(\in \{(9, 21), (12, 26), (20, 50)\}\)
\end{itemize}

Sub-daily timeframes hold \texttt{bear\_alloc} fixed at 1.0 (full long / full short), as the bear-allocation dimension is a 1d-only concern in our framework.

For each (ticker, timeframe) combination, we sweep all parameter combinations, compute Sharpe, total return, max drawdown, and number of trades, and save to CSV.

\section{Phase 3 results: synthetic-market null control}

We test whether Archer's edge is specific to certain market structures by running it against 6 synthetic market types (50 random seeds each, 2500 candles per simulation):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Pure GBM} ($\mu=0$, $\sigma=0.02$): pure random walk.
\item \textbf{Trending (mild)} (drift=10\%/yr, vol=15\%/yr).
\item \textbf{Trending (strong)} (drift=20\%/yr, vol=20\%/yr).
\item \textbf{Mean-reverting} (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, speed=0.05, $\sigma=0.02$).
\item \textbf{Regime-switching} (Markov chain: bull/bear/crash).
\item \textbf{Block bootstrap from SPY returns} (preserves volatility clustering).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Market type} & \textbf{Archer mean} & \textbf{B\&H mean} & \textbf{Edge} & \textbf{Winner} \\

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why vanilla EMA-cross wins on Sharpe}

Across all 9 tickers and the full default grid, the parameterization that maximizes Sharpe is \((n_\mu=1, x=1.0, \text{EMA }9/21)\)---the plain vanilla EMA-cross with no delay. This is because \(x=1.0\) forces the delay to always be zero, which is the optimal entry timing for capturing trend transitions.

Archer's value is not in Sharpe but in \textbf{return and robustness}. The stochastic delay reduces whipsaw trades by skipping signals where the fast EMA would reverse within a few candles, which lowers drawdown volatility but also smooths out some returns.

\subsection{When does the delay help?}

The synthetic null control reveals that the delay helps specifically in regime-switching markets---where the price oscillates between trending and ranging. In monotonic uptrends, the delay introduces friction (you enter slightly late); in regime switches, the delay protects against false breakouts.

This is consistent with the walk-forward finding that the strategy's best parameterization varies window-to-window: in trend-friendly periods it picks vanilla; in regime-heavy periods it picks delay.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item The 1h timeframe was not tested in this paper (Yahoo Finance 1h data is limited to 730 days; ccxt crypto 1h has more history but was deferred).
\item Transaction costs were modeled at a flat 5 bps per side. Real-world slippage may be higher for crypto and lower for highly liquid ETFs.
\item The walk-forward uses a fixed 252/63 day split; varying this split would change the test results (cf.\ QuantGuild Lecture 97).
\item Short-selling was modeled with cash settlement (no borrow fees or margin requirements).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

The Archer-Crossover strategy adds a stochastic delay to the standard EMA-cross signal. The delay is parameterized by \(n_\mu\) (mean delay), \(x\) (probability of zero delay), and \(\sigma_n\) (delay std). Across 9 tickers and 4860 parameter combinations:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Best zone:} \(n_\mu=3\), \(x=0.75\), EMA (9, 21), with median Sharpe 1.03 across tickers.
\item \textbf{Robust Sharpe:} 56--98 (vs.\ 1.0 threshold for stability).
\item \textbf{Walk-forward:} 80\% positive out-of-sample Sharpe on average across 320+ windows.
\item \textbf{Specialization:} Wins on regime-switching markets (+399 pp vs B\&H), ties on pure random, loses on monotonic trends.
\end{itemize}

The strategy is a real-world deployable adaptation of the EMA-cross that adds noise-filtering in regime-heavy periods without sacrificing trend-following performance. The full implementation, sweep results, and validation are open-source at \url{https://github.com/IkkeOmar/archer-crossover}.
